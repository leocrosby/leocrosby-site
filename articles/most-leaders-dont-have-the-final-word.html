Most Leaders Don't Have the Final Word

Leo Crosby  ·  July 22, 2026

Most leadership advice is written for the person at the top. I get why. Founders and CEOs and senior pastors have the biggest stage, so they get the biggest stack of books written about them. But here's the thing: most leaders don't sit in that seat.

I don't either.

I've spent almost two decades as a pastor working underneath someone else's vision, not because I lacked ambition, but because that's genuinely the seat I'm wired for. And I've met a lot of people in that same kind of seat, whatever their title happens to be: department head, executive pastor, vice president, chief of staff, director, senior manager, campus leader. We're the ones translating vision into an actual Tuesday. We absorb pressure from above and expectations from below. We get held responsible for results we don't fully control, in decisions we don't always get the final word on.

And if I'm honest, there were seasons early on where I quietly believed my role was smaller because it wasn't the top one. I don't believe that anymore. This series is about why.

The job nobody writes books about

Walk into any bookstore's leadership section and it's the same vantage point over and over: the founder, the CEO, the head coach. How to cast vision. How to build culture. How to make the hard call when everyone's looking at you.

Good material. Just not written for most of the people actually doing leadership work.

Every organization of any real size runs on a much bigger group: the leaders underneath the top seat who make the vision real. We're in the room when the decision gets made. We're just not the one who makes it.

If you're in one of those seats, you already know this tension, even if you've never put words to it. Responsibility without full authority. Expected to stay aligned without always being asked whether you agree. Real influence, but no ownership. You do the execution, and somebody else often gets the credit. And you're getting squeezed from both directions, pressure from above and expectations from below, sometimes in the same afternoon.

I think about the department head who watches a decision get made in a leadership meeting she wasn't fully sold on, then walks straight into a room with her own team and has to make it land, without throwing the decision-makers under the bus and without pretending she loved it. Or the chief of staff who sees a problem forming three weeks before it ever reaches his boss's desk, and has to decide: raise it now, sit on it, or quietly go fix it himself. Neither one of them holds the final word. Both of them are doing real leadership, right now, today, and neither one shows up in a book written for the person at the top.

Handle that tension badly and it curdles into something we've all seen up close: frustration, office politics, quiet resentment, disengagement, eventually burnout. Handle it well and it becomes something rarer: trust that compounds, stability that outlasts any one leader, an organization that multiplies instead of just executes.

The difference was never the seat. It's whether the person in it understands what the seat is actually for.

Where influence actually lives

Here's an assumption worth pushing on: that influence is a function of title, and the higher your seat, the more you shape what actually happens.

I've watched enough organizations up close to know that's just not true. Vision gets set at the top. But vision doesn't build anything by itself. It gets built, or quietly abandoned, by the leaders underneath it, in a hundred small decisions a week.

A strategy is only as good as the department head who commits to it instead of slow-walking it. A culture is only as real as the manager who models it in a hard conversation, or lets it slip because the model was inconvenient that day. The senior leader casts the vision once, in a room, on a good day. Everyone beneath them either builds it or erodes it, in decisions nobody's watching.

Think about the healthiest organization you've ever been part of. I'd bet what you actually remember isn't the person at the very top. It's a layer of leaders just beneath them. The manager who fought for you in a room you weren't in. The person who took the heat for a decision they didn't even make, so their team didn't have to. That's where influence actually lives.

What I'm not saying

I want to be clear about something, because I don't want this series mistaken for a case for keeping your head down. It isn't. Blind loyalty and passive support produce exactly the disengagement they're trying to avoid. You can't stay quiet for years and call it commitment.

What I am making a case for is harder than either compliance or complaint. Call it disciplined influence: making the leader above you stronger instead of competing with them, telling the truth even when agreement would be easier, treating the people below you like individuals instead of roles, caring more about results than explanations, and holding all of that steady for years without letting it curdle into resentment.

That's a harder skill set than "go along with everything" or "push back on everything." It's also the actual job for most of us.

What's coming in this series

Over the next several weeks, I want to walk through what disciplined influence looks like in practice. Not as an abstract virtue, but as a set of specific, learnable moves.

Aligning deeply with the leader above you without losing your own voice. Strengthening that leader without shrinking yourself to do it. Practicing courageous candor instead of silent compliance, so agreement actually means something because disagreement was always allowed. Leading the people on your team as individuals instead of interchangeable roles. Focusing on results instead of explanations when things go sideways. Empowering the people you lead to take real risks and fail without you hovering over every decision. And doing all of that long enough, and steadily enough, that it doesn't quietly turn into resentment somewhere around year three.

None of this is complicated. All of it is hard to sustain for years in a seat that rarely gets recognized publicly. That's exactly why I think it's worth taking seriously.

The reframe

If you're reading this from one of those in-between seats, here's the shift I'd invite you to make.

Stop measuring your leadership against the person who holds the final word. You're not failing to be them. You're doing a job with its own demands and its own weight, and it deserves its own definition of success. I've started thinking of it less as "just supporting" the person above me, and more as structurally strengthening the whole organization from where I stand. Support is passive. Strengthening is active. One shrinks you. The other grows you, and grows everything around you.

Influence isn't positional. It's practiced. Very few of us will ever hold the final word. Almost all of the leadership that actually matters happens in the seats that don't.

If that's your seat, I think it has more power in it than you've probably given it credit for. I'll spend the rest of this series showing you why, starting with a myth that quietly convinces good leaders their seat doesn't count.
